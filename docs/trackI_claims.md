## When quantum approaches helped in these experiments

The simulated query-count comparison shows fewer oracle calls for Grover than exhaustive linear search at the recorded sizes. This is a query-count comparison under the specified oracle model, not a measured hardware runtime result. The saved Track C and Track I classification results do not show a quantum model beating the best classical model in the paired test splits.

## When they did not help and why

The QML runs used small datasets and classical statevector/kernel simulation. The best classical model was at least as accurate on every paired split. The shot-based quantum kernels were less accurate on average than the exact kernel on all three datasets. The Grover statevector simulator carries an amplitude vector whose size grows as `2^n` and applies about `sqrt(N)` iterations, so its measured runtime is not a quantum search runtime. The noise and barren-plateau studies are simulations; their CSVs contain reduced success under noise and small gradient variances in some settings, but do not support broad hardware or scaling conclusions. Data loading and fault-tolerant hardware costs are not measured.

## Contrast with parallel computing

The Track D strong-scaling rows report measured speedups of 1.000, 1.232, 1.985, 2.215 and 3.262 at 1, 2, 4, 8 and 12 processors, respectively. The corresponding Grover query-reduction factors at `N=2^p` are theoretical (1.801, 2.546, 5.093, 20.372 and 81.487). These compare different resources and cost models: parallel speedup is measured on this platform and cannot exceed the processor count in the ideal model; Grover's factors are theoretical oracle-query reductions and assume a suitable oracle.
